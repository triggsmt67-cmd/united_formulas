## The spec your machine was built to, and the water you actually have

Hobart, one of the premier manufacturers of commercial kitchen dish machines, recommends a water hardness of 3.5 grains per gallon (gpg) or less. When water climbs past 7 grains per gallon, equipment builders classify it as hard water that requires chemical water conditioning or softening.

The City of Great Falls Water Treatment Plant on River Drive North draws surface water directly from the Missouri River, just below where the Sun River enters. The city plant filters sediment, removes river organics, and disinfects the water, but it does not soften it. The city publishes an annual hardness range between 127 and 167 milligrams per liter (mg/L). When you divide that by the industry standard conversion factor of 17.1, Great Falls tap water runs between 7.4 and 9.8 grains per gallon. It averages around 9.4 grains per gallon, or roughly 160 ppm.

This means commercial dish machines running on city water in Great Falls operate at more than double, and sometimes nearly triple, the maximum hardness Hobart recommends.

This is not a sales claim from a chemical vendor. You can verify it yourself in five minutes by comparing the manufacturer specification tag on your dish machine with the annual drinking water quality report published by the City of Great Falls.

Local river geology also creates a seasonal swing that catches kitchen managers off guard. During late spring runoff in May and June, fresh snowmelt from the Big Belt and Little Belt mountain ranges rushes down the Missouri, temporarily diluting the river down near 7.4 grains per gallon. By January and February, river flow drops under winter ice, groundwater recharge increases, and mineral hardness climbs toward 9.8 grains per gallon. If your chemical rep only checks your dispenser once in the summer, your machine will be starved of cleaning power all winter long.

## What hardness actually does inside a commercial dish pit

When water carries 9.4 grains of dissolved limestone into a high-temperature kitchen machine, three distinct problems occur.

### 1. It destroys detergent before cleaning begins
Calcium and magnesium carry positive electrical charges. The cleaning agents in commercial warewash detergents, called anionic surfactants, carry negative electrical charges. 

When your dispenser pumps detergent into hard wash water, opposite charges attract immediately. The calcium bonds to the surfactant molecules and turns them into an insoluble curd. This is the exact same chemical reaction that causes soap scum on bathroom tile. Every grain of hardness in your water binds up and wastes active cleaning chemistry you already paid for. Your dirty dishes only get whatever leftover chemistry survives the reaction.

### 2. It bakes scale onto heating coils and spray jets
Commercial dish machines wash dishes between 150°F and 160°F, and high-temp booster heaters push final rinse water between 180°F and 190°F to sanitize without chemicals.

At temperatures above 140°F, dissolved calcium bicarbonate breaks down. It turns into solid calcium carbonate, which falls out of the water and crystallizes onto hot metal. It coats your booster element, wash tank coils, and internal water pipes with a chalky white crust. 

Scale acts as a thermal insulator. Just a 1/16-inch crust of scale on a heating coil forces the element to run significantly hotter to warm the water, driving up kitchen utility bills by 11% to 15% and burning out elements years early. Flakes of hard scale also break loose from tank walls, travel through the wash pump, and lodge inside the small orifices of the spray arms. That drops wash pressure and stops the rotating arms from spinning freely.

### 3. It turns clean glasses into cloudy, etched glasses
That chalky white haze on restaurant bar glasses is mineral scale, not trapped food starch. 

If you leave hard water on hot glassware, the problem gets worse over time. High heat and alkaline wash water cause the minerals to react directly with the silica in the glass. This creates permanent microscopic surface damage called chemical etching. Once glassware gets etched, acid delimers cannot fix it. The only solution is throwing the glasses in the trash and buying new inventory.

## The label dilution assumes water you do not have

Chemical companies print dilution ratios like 1:64 or 1:128 on their jugs. Those numbers were calculated in out-of-state testing laboratories using pure, softened water with zero mineral interference.

When you run that same detergent in Great Falls on 9.4 grain water, you must feed more chemical concentrate into the tank just to satisfy the mineral demand and still clean grease off plates. A 5-gallon pail that claims it will wash 3,000 racks may only wash 1,800 to 2,000 racks once properly dosed for local water.

## Practical shop-floor steps: three checks in ten minutes

1. **Test at your dish machine, not from the city website.** Water sitting in older commercial plumbing lines along 10th Avenue South, downtown Central Avenue, or the Northwest Bypass can pick up additional scale before reaching the wash bay. [Test strips take two minutes](/contact?request=audit) and provide an immediate reading.
2. **Use the quick vinegar test.** If you want to confirm whether white buildup inside your tank is food residue or hard water scale, scrape a small flake off the wall and place a drop of vinegar or acid delimer on it. If it fizzes immediately, it is calcium carbonate from the Missouri River.
3. **Write the measured hardness number directly on the machine.** Write the measured grains per gallon and the date on a strip of tape on the front of the dish machine with a permanent marker. That way every line cook, kitchen manager, and service tech knows the baseline water condition.
4. **Test active wash tank concentration, not dispenser dial settings.** A click-stop dial or speed knob on an injection pump does not tell you what concentration reaches the spray nozzles. Dip a chemical test strip directly into the wash tank during an active cycle to verify active ppm.

## One thing you do not need to worry about

Your quat sanitizer works fine on Great Falls municipal tap water.

Quaternary ammonium sanitizers used in third-sink bar rinses and red kitchen wipe buckets are sensitive to extreme water hardness. However, EPA regulations and manufacturer specifications rate standard quat formulas to remain stable in water up to 500 mg/L (ppm) hardness. Great Falls municipal water peaks around 167 mg/L. Anyone claiming that city tap water is deactivating your red-bucket sanitizer is trying to sell you extra chemical additives you do not need. Keep your attention on detergent dosing, scale control, and booster temperatures.

## Common Mistakes

- Running cloudy glassware through the machine a second time instead of fixing water hardness.
- Relying on the dilution ratio printed on the jug label without adjusting for local minerals.
- Checking the dispenser knob position and assuming it matches the chemical strength in the tank.
- Acid deliming the machine once a month while ignoring the daily wash water that created the scale.
- Relying on the city's annual average hardness instead of testing your own tap with a strip.
- Blaming the dish machine or buying a replacement booster heater when untreated water caused the failure.
- Hand-drying clean glassware with bar towels to wipe off mineral haze, which spreads bacteria and violates sanitation codes.
